# engine/simulation

`simulate(network, packet)` and its trace: the stage pipeline, rule matching and where a packet stops; and `simulateSequence(network, sends)` (`sequence.ts`), which runs a list of packets in order, each at its own second of the game clock, and carries what one leaves for the next.

- Each step carries a reason code, never a sentence; `src/copy/` turns codes into Spanish.
- Outcomes (`OUTCOMES`, the list the incident contract reads): `replied`, `timeout`, `refused`, `arrived-no-reply`, `unresolved`, `invalid-address`, `no-route`, `unreachable`, `ttl-exceeded` and `lost`. In a VPC a packet is lost when no instance has its IP; anywhere it is lost when it goes from the internet to a private IP (RFC 1918); in an office with routers it is also lost when a client actor sends it to a public IP that no router owns. A device that sends a packet to its own IP, or to any `127.x.x.x` loopback address (RFC 1122, §3.2.1.3), answers itself, past no firewall.
- The `dns` stage turns a name into an IP (`stages/dns.ts`, `createDnsStage(send)`). A device of an office looks a name up the way the packet says (`PacketSpec.lookup`, see `src/engine/dns/CLAUDE.md`). `system`, the default and what `ping` and `nc` do, reads the hosts file of the device first (`dns-hosts-file`: nothing is sent) and then asks its resolver (`Device.dnsServer`): `resolve` sends the question as a UDP datagram to port 53 through `sendQuestion` (`simulate.ts`), a datagram of the same send that meets the firewalls, routes, NAT and services like any other packet, and then asks the resolver for the name, from its cache or from its authoritative servers. The answer is a step with the reason `dns-answered` (the IP, and `canonical` when the name was an alias), `dns-cached` (the same, from the cache of the resolver, with the seconds `ttlLeft`), `dns-nxdomain`, `dns-servfail` or `dns-no-address` (a name that exists and holds no address); all but the first two end the packet as `unresolved`. A question that got no answer ends as `unresolved` with one of two reasons that never share a case: `dns-resolver-silent` (the question never reached a host: a firewall or a router dropped it, a router or the sender warned, nobody has the IP; no resolver spoke) and `dns-no-resolver` (a host got the question and did not answer it as a resolver, because it is no resolver and answered anyway or because it rejected the datagram with Port Unreachable, and the reason names that host as `hostId`); the warning that came back, if one did, is in the trace of the question (`Trace.question`) and not in the ping's. A device with no `dnsServer` keeps the step it always had, `dns-unresolved`, because chapter 1 tests pin it. Every other sender (a device of a VPC, an actor) resolves EC2 names as before and gets no `Trace.dns`. `sendQuestion` runs the question with the NAT table, the port counters and the second of the send that asks, and hands the table and the counters back: the question takes the next source port of the sender and leaves its row before the packet that asked, and the question is kept as `Trace.question` (`{ packet, trace }`, see `src/engine/dns/CLAUDE.md`), while it writes no step, hop or leg in the trace of the packet. Whatever a resolver answers is stored in its cache, whoever asked. `Trace.dns` is `{ name, status, answers, soa?, fromCache, ttlLeft?, usedHostsFile, viaDig }`, present when an office device looked a name up (also when it had no resolver: `NO-RESOLVER`) and absent when the destination was an IP, in a VPC and for an actor; `answers` hold the aliases first and then the addresses, each with its TTL (the seconds it has left, when `fromCache`), `ttlLeft` exists only when `fromCache` and is the seconds of the record that expires first, a hosts-file answer has no `answers` (the address is `resolvedIp`) and a negative answer carries the SOA of its zone with the TTL of RFC 2308 §3. A trace with a name has the same `steps` as before except for the dns step, and a packet that asked its resolver carries the question in `Trace.question`. The tests that pin the legacy step are in `simulate.test.ts`.
- A `dig` (`PacketSpec.lookup` `dig`, a UDP datagram to port 53 with the name as `to`) is the datagram to the resolver itself, not a lookup that goes on to the address it finds. The `dns` stage sets its destination to the `dnsServer` of the sender (`ctx.destIp`) and remembers the name (`ctx.asking`); the packet goes through `route`, the firewalls, `forward`, `host` and `reply` like any other, so its trace is the trip of the question: `resolvedIp` and `destinationId` are the resolver's, `header` has the ports (the source port of a sequence, 53), `exchange` is `query` and `response`, and a host with no service on port 53 answers Port Unreachable, which is its `Trace.notice` and its outcome `unreachable`. When the trip ends, `conclude` (`simulate.ts`) asks `answerFrom` (`src/engine/dns/resolve.ts`) with the finished trace: if it came back `replied` from a server that is a resolver, the resolver answers the name (cache first, then its authoritative servers) and the trace gains `dns`; any other trace gains `dns` with `NO-RESOLVER`. NXDOMAIN and SERVFAIL are answers, so the dig is `replied` with that `status`. A dig writes no `dns` step and leaves its own row in the translation table, because it is a send of the player. A `dig` of a device with no resolver ends as a lookup with none; a `dig` whose `to` is already an IP is the datagram to that IP with no `dns`.
- The `address` stage runs first. Four numbers that are not an IPv4 address (one above 255) end as `invalid-address` before any lookup; `192.168.1`, with the wrong number of groups, is a name and goes on to `dns`. The name `localhost` becomes `127.0.0.1` with no lookup (RFC 6761, §6.3). A valid address or a name writes no step there, so a trace that does not use them is the same as before.
- In an office, a device that cannot send ends as `no-route` with no warning: it has no cable (`route-not-connected`) or no default gateway for an IP outside its LAN (`route-no-gateway`). An IP that nobody connected has in the sender's LAN, or a gateway that nobody has, ends as `unreachable`: the sender's own device warns, and `Trace.notice` says `host-unreachable` from the sender (`forward-no-owner`). A gateway that is not a router drops the packet in silence, so it ends as `timeout` (`route-gateway-not-a-router`). The sender's outbound firewall comes first: it silences a packet to an IP nobody has, and then no warning comes back.
- Routers (`paths/routers.ts`): an office with `routers` carries a packet outside its LAN through them. Every router the packet reaches first asks whether the destination is one of its own interfaces: if so it answers (`forward-router-replies`), whatever the TTL, and the packet goes on to `host` and `reply`. Otherwise it takes 1 from the TTL (`packet.ttl`, `DEFAULT_TTL` = 64 by default; RFC 1812, §5.3.1), adds a `Trace.hops` entry and, at 0, discards the packet and warns with `time-exceeded` (`forward-ttl-exceeded`, outcome `ttl-exceeded`). Then `onwardFrom` (`src/engine/network/addressing/routing.ts`) picks the next stop: a device of a LAN it faces (`forward-hop`, then the firewall of the device), a server behind it (it answers like a device with no firewall and no programs), another router, or a failure. A failure is `forward-private-dropped` (a private IP at a router outside the office: silent, `timeout`), `forward-no-route` (`net-unreachable`) or `forward-no-host` (`host-unreachable`, the router has the network connected and nobody has the IP). The last two warn only if the router has `sendsUnreachable`; otherwise they are silent, and the outcome is `timeout`. A router whose table has no route that covers the destination is the case of `forward-no-route`: the office router after `removeRoute` of its only route warns `net-unreachable` from its interface towards the sender (`Trace.notice`, outcome `unreachable`) for any destination outside the networks it has connected, a private IP included, since a router that faces a LAN never takes the `private` branch. With only the `default` route back, a private IP goes on to the first router outside the office and dies there in silence (`forward-private-dropped`, as in chapter 1): `onwardFrom` is not changed by the routes the player edits (decided 11 of chapter 2). The tests `a router with no route that covers the destination warns Destination Net Unreachable`, `with only a default route, a private destination is dropped by the provider with no warning, as in chapter 1` and `a more specific route beats the default route and the trace says so` (`routing-table.test.ts`) build their worlds with `removeRoute` and `addRoute`. The walk is bounded by the TTL, so a loop between two routers ends when it runs out.
- `Trace.hops` lists every router that took the packet, in order, with the TTL left after it. The router that discards at TTL 0 is in the list (`ttlLeft` 0); the router that answers for itself is not (it took no TTL). A hop's `ip`, and the source of every warning a router sends, is the router's interface towards the sender (RFC 1812, §4.3.2.4): the one on the sender's LAN, or else the one the way back to the sender leaves through. So in a loop the same router always warns from the same IP.
- A router or a server that answers is `ctx.responder` and the trace's `destinationId` is its id; `receiverOf` gives `host` and `reply` the same shape for a device and for a responder. Only a device writes tcpdump lines. A responder has no firewall. A router has no services, so a TCP packet to it ends as `refused` and a UDP datagram to it as `unreachable`; a server of the internet has the `services` of its actor (`Responder.services`, which `receiverOf` turns into the `host` of the receiver), so a TCP packet or a UDP datagram to it is accepted on a port it serves and refused (RST or Port Unreachable) on any other. The reply of a ping never goes through a firewall.
- Leaving the office writes `route-leaves-office` and no step of NAT: the source stays the private IP in the steps, in `effectiveSource` and in `Trace.header`. What the NAT of the office did with the source is a field of its own, `Trace.translation` (see below), never a step or a reason.
- `Trace.header` is the packet's header in an office: `request` (`source` = the sender's private IP, always, even when the packet leaves the office, because the way out is a black box, except for a packet a device sends to a loopback address, which leaves with `127.0.0.1` (a ping to `127.5.5.5` is `127.0.0.1` → `127.5.5.5`, its reply `127.5.5.5` → `127.0.0.1`; `localhost` is `127.0.0.1` → `127.0.0.1`), while a ping to the device's own IP keeps its own IP; `destination` = the resolved IP, so `localhost` shows `127.0.0.1`; `ttl` = `packet.ttl` or `DEFAULT_TTL`) and, only when the packet was answered (`replied`), `reply`: the same two addresses swapped (RFC 792). A packet that stopped at its sender (`invalid-address`, an unresolved name, `no-route`) has no header. A TCP or UDP packet also carries `sourcePort` and `destinationPort` in `request`: the source port is `PacketSpec.sourcePort` (an integer from 1 to 65535) or, when the packet gives none, the next port of its sender (the `port` stage gives it, see below; 51514 for the first packet of a loose `simulate`), and the destination port is `packet.port`; the `reply` swaps them with the addresses. An ICMP message carries no ports, even if its spec gives a `sourcePort`.
- `Trace.exchange` lists what the two ends of a TCP or UDP packet said to each other, as `{ from: 'client' | 'server', kind }` (`client` sent the packet, `server` is who it was for), in order. The opening is the packet itself, so it is there from the start (`openingOf` in `context.ts` seeds `ctx.exchange`): `SYN` for TCP, `query` for UDP. `host` adds the `RST` of a refusal (`sayRefusal`) and `reply` adds the answer (`sayAnswer`): `SYN-ACK` then the client's `ACK` for TCP, `response` for UDP. So an accepted connection is SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK; a refused one, SYN, RST; and a packet nobody answered (a firewall of the receiver dropped it, a router warned, it timed out) is only the opening, SYN or query. A packet that the outbound firewall of its own sender drops never left the device, so `firewallOutStage` takes the opening back and the trace has no `exchange` (its header stays: it is the packet the sender built). A UDP datagram that a service takes is answered, so its exchange is `query` and `response`; one that nobody takes gets a Port Unreachable, which is an ICMP warning and not a UDP message, so its exchange is just `query`, like the one a firewall silenced. The exchange exists exactly where the header does and only for TCP and UDP: it is absent from an ICMP message, from a packet that stopped at its sender and from a VPC (the stages still record it there, and `finish` leaves it out). No stage writes a step for it, so the steps of every trace are the same.
- `simulateSequence(net, sends)` (`sequence.ts`) takes `sends`, an ordered list of `{ packet, at }` (`at` = seconds of the game clock, as given: it neither sorts nor checks them), and returns one trace per send, in the same order. It folds the sends over a `Carried` it creates inside the call, so the same sends always give the same traces and no module keeps anything between calls; it assigns no port and decides nothing, it only hands what one send leaves to the next. `run(net, packet, carried, at)` (`simulate.ts`) is the pipeline with an entry that takes what the sends before it left and returns the trace with what it leaves, measured against second `at` (`ctx.now`); `simulate` is `run` with nothing carried and the clock at 0 (`GAME_START`), the sequence of one send. `Carried` is `{ nextSourcePort, nat, dnsCache, apps? }`: `nextSourcePort` holds, for each sender by id, the ephemeral source port its next TCP or UDP packet leaves from, `nat` the rows of the translation table, and `dnsCache` holds, for each resolver by id, the entries its answers put there (`src/engine/dns/cache.ts`); the initial `dns.cache` of a resolver is data of the world and is read beside it, against the same clock; `apps`, absent until an app changes its orders, holds the orders of each app (`Apps`, `src/engine/web/CLAUDE.md`). The question of a send by name runs with the same `Carried` of the send that asks and hands it back (`sendQuestion`), so it is a datagram of the sequence: it takes its port and leaves its row before the packet. A TCP or UDP send that ends before the `port` stage (an address that is not valid, a name that nobody resolved) takes no port of its own; one that ends later (no route, a firewall) still does.
- `cacheAfter(net, sends, resolverId, now)` (`sequence.ts`) is what the card of a resolver's cache reads: it replays the sends exactly as `simulateSequence` does (one shared `play`) and returns the entries the resolver with that id holds at second `now` of the game clock (`entriesOf`: its own `dns.cache`, with what the sends stored replacing the entry of the same name and type), each with `ttlLeft` = `expiresAt` less `now`, and none that ran out (`now < expiresAt`, as in `cachedAnswer`). It reads no clock and keeps nothing between calls; for an id that is no resolver it is empty. It adds nothing to the traces, so `simulate` and the golden traces of chapter 4 do not change. The caller leaves out the sends that ran no packet (a traceroute is not in the sequence).
- The source port (`port` stage, `stages/port.ts`, which also holds `withSourcePort`): each sender, by `packet.from.id`, has one counter, `Carried.nextSourcePort`, that starts at `DEFAULT_SOURCE_PORT` and adds one for each TCP or UDP packet it sends that gives no `sourcePort`, whatever the protocol and the destination (the port of a new exchange changes). The stage runs after `dns`, once the destination is an IP, and the question of a lookup by name takes its port from the same counter before the packet does: the question 51514 and an `nc` 51515. A loose `simulate` is the sequence of one send, so a packet that asks nobody leaves from 51514 and one that asks leaves the 51514 to its question and goes out from 51515. A sender is any endpoint, a device or a client actor of the internet (ids are unique across both, `checkIds`): a client also opens its connections from an ephemeral port. A packet with its own `sourcePort` keeps it and leaves the counter as it was, like a port a program binds by hand instead of asking the system for one; the counter does not skip a port a given one used, because the sends are separate exchanges and nothing keeps a port bound. An ICMP message has no ports: it is passed through as it is and uses none. The counter does not wrap: the 14,023rd TCP or UDP send of one sender would get 65536, which is not a port, and no session of the game gets there. In a VPC the assigned port shows in no trace, because the header is only drawn in an office.
- `host` looks a TCP packet or a UDP datagram up with `lookupService` (`src/engine/network/services.ts`) among the services of the receiver, by protocol, port and the IP the packet is for (`ctx.destIp`). `accepts` writes `host-accepts` and goes on to `reply`: a TCP connection is answered with SYN-ACK and ACK, a datagram with a `response`. For TCP, `closed` writes `host-refused` and `wrong-address` writes `host-wrong-address` (with the `ip`, which is the destination the packet had, for a service that listens only on the loopback address): both answer with a reset (`sayRefusal`, a tcpdump line `TCP <port> RST` at a device) and end as `refused`, with the exchange SYN and RST. `stopped` (the services on that port belong to processes that are not running) writes `process-stopped`, with the device and the port, and answers like a closed port in both protocols. For UDP, `closed` writes `host-udp-closed` and `wrong-address` writes `host-wrong-address` (the same reason as for TCP): both answer with ICMP Port Unreachable (`rejectUdp`), which is `Trace.notice` `{ kind: 'port-unreachable', fromId: the host, fromIp: the IP the datagram was sent to }` (`sendNotice`, the same one the routers use), a tcpdump line `ICMP udp port <port> unreachable` at a device, and the outcome `unreachable`. RFC 1122, §4.1.3.1 (read on 2026-10-11): "If a datagram arrives addressed to a UDP port for which there is no pending LISTEN call, UDP SHOULD send an ICMP Port Unreachable message." The warning comes from the host, never from a router on the way: the trace still lists those routers in `hops`, and the warning travels back as any other one, past no firewall. The game sets no rate limit (a real kernel can limit how many it sends) and does not model the cases where the RFC lets a host stay silent. A UDP datagram to a `loopback` service at another IP is answered the same way (reasoned like TCP, unverified, `src/engine/network/CLAUDE.md`). The firewalls run before `host`, so a firewall that drops the packet answers nothing and the host never sees it. The reply of an accepted connection or datagram is never filtered, as the reply of a ping. The listening address is unverified (`src/engine/network/CLAUDE.md`). A VPC runs the same stage: a datagram that reaches an instance with no UDP service ends as `unreachable` there too, though no flow or probe of chapter 4 gets through a Security Group with UDP.
- `Trace.rttMs` is the round trip time of an office, and exists only when something came back to the sender: `replied`, `refused`, `unreachable` from a router or from a host (Port Unreachable) and `ttl-exceeded`; silence has none. It is twice the sum of the legs the packet travelled, recorded by `addLeg` where each is crossed: the sender's LAN (`forward`, once someone in it takes the packet), every router the packet reaches (the one that answers or warns included, since the warning travels from it), the destination's LAN when a router hands the packet to a device, and the server. Nothing past whoever answered or warned counts. A trip that never left the device (`127.x.x.x`, `localhost`, its own IP) has no legs and takes `HOST_ONLY_RTT_MS` (0.05 ms), smaller than any trip that crosses a wire. A `host-unreachable` the sender generates itself has no `rttMs`: on Linux it comes only after the laptop waited for someone to answer for that IP, and ping prints no `time=` on that line. A Port Unreachable a device sends itself (a datagram to `127.x.x.x` or to its own IP) does have one, `HOST_ONLY_RTT_MS`, because the host did receive the datagram. All the latencies are illustrative, not measured.
- `traceroute(network, from, to, maxHops)` (`paths/traceroute.ts`) is built on `simulate`: one Echo Request per TTL from 1 to `maxHops` (like `tracert`, and `traceroute -I` on Linux; no UDP, which belongs to chapter 2), so it adds nothing to the engine's stages. A line is `{ hop, ip?, rttMs?, unreachable?, reached }`: `hop` is the TTL, `ip` is the source of the warning (`Trace.notice.fromIp`) or, when the destination answered, the destination, and `rttMs` is the trace's. A probe nobody answered (`timeout`, `lost`) is a line with no `ip` and no `rttMs`; the view draws a `*`. A probe that ends in a Destination Host or Network Unreachable gives one last line with the IP that warned and `unreachable: 'host' | 'net'` (BSD `traceroute(8)`, the macOS page read on 2026-10-10: `!H` and `!N` mark host and network unreachable, and "if almost all the probes result in some kind of unreachable, traceroute will give up and exit"): it keeps the trace's `rttMs` for a router, and has none when the sender generated the warning itself. So it ends at the destination (`reached`), at that line, or at `maxHops`; a loop between two routers fills every line, alternating their IPs. `TRACEROUTE_MAX_HOPS` = 8 is a number of the game, not that of any system. When the first probe never leaves the sender (`invalid-address`, `unresolved`, `no-route`) there are no lines and `localError` is its trace. `maxHops` outside 1 to 255 (the TTL range) throws a `RangeError`.
- The `route` stage decides with the routing table of the sender, which `routingTableOf` derives from the network (`src/engine/network/addressing/routing-table.ts`; nothing stores it): `chooseRoute` picks the row that covers the destination with the longest range, the same rule the routers use (`coveringRoutes`). The connected row of the LAN of the device delivers straight to the destination, with no next hop; the default row hands the packet to the gateway, which is where a destination in another LAN of the office, or outside it, goes. A device with no gateway has no default row, so a destination outside its LAN has no row and ends as `route-no-gateway`. The steps, reasons and outcomes are the same as when the stage used three conditions: the test `the choice of route in the office of chapter 1 gives the same outcome as before` checks it against that old rule for every device of every office world of chapter 1.
- `Trace.routes` lists the routes the packet was carried by: one `RouteDecision` (`holderId`, the `route` chosen, its `kind` and the `alsoCovered` rows that also covered the destination and lost) for the sender and one for each router that forwarded it, in the order the packet met them. It is a field of its own: the decision writes no step and no reason, so the steps of every trace are the same. The sender records its decision where it hands the packet to the network (`handToNetwork`), so a packet that stays at its sender (no cable, no gateway for an outside IP), one to a loopback address or to the device's own IP, one that stopped at the `address` stage and one that leaves a routerless office have no decision of the sender. A router records its choice where it forwards (`forwardOn` in `paths/routers.ts`), also when the next stop turns out to be a host nobody has (`forward-no-host`); the router that answers for itself, the one that discards at TTL 0, the one that drops a private IP and the one with no route that covers the destination (`forward-no-route`) record nothing. A VPC has no routing table in the model, so its traces never carry the field.
- `Trace.translation` is what the NAT of the office did with the source of the packet (`src/engine/nat/CLAUDE.md`): protocol, the end it left the device with (`inside`), the end it leaves the office with (`outside`), `isNew` (this packet made the row) and `fixed` (true when a port forward delivered the packet). A router translates where it sends the packet out of the office: `forwardOn` (`paths/routers.ts`) calls `translateOnExit` (`stages/nat.ts`) when the next stop is a router or a server, once per trip. It happens only for a packet from a device of the office, through a router that faces a LAN, by an interface whose IP is not private; the link to another office, a packet that stays inside the office, one the first router discards for its TTL or finds no route for, one that stopped at its sender and any packet of a VPC carry none. A packet that dies farther down the road (the TTL ends at the provider, a private IP is dropped there) was translated, and its row exists. A ping is translated with no ports and no row. The field writes no step, reason, hop or leg and changes no other field, so the steps of every trace are the same as before. The table that `translateSource` extends is `ctx.nat`: a loose `simulate` starts it empty, `simulateSequence` carries it.
- A packet from the internet to an office with routers (decided 17 of chapter 2): a client actor (`you`, `attacker`, `home`) can send to a public IP. `route` names the router that owns the IP (`ctx.entry`) and `forward` enters it, with the TTL going down once. No router owns the IP: `route-no-device` at the network and the outcome `lost`. A private IP from outside is lost earlier (`route-private-from-outside`), and an office with no routers keeps `route-no-public-device` and `timeout`. The router answers for itself (`forward-router-replies`) every packet to one of its own interfaces, a ping to the public IP of the office router included; only a TCP or UDP packet to a public IP of a router that faces a LAN is the NAT's (`entersNat`, `src/engine/nat/CLAUDE.md`). That router takes 1 from the TTL and adds its `Trace.hops` entry before it looks, so a packet whose TTL ends there is discarded with Time Exceeded and translated by nobody. It then looks for a fixed forward or a row of `ctx.nat`. Found: it writes `nat-forwarded` (stage `forward`, at the router), sets `Trace.translation` (`fixed` for a forward) and the translated destination `ctx.delivered`, and goes on as any router does with a packet for a device (`forward-hop`, the route it picked for the device's IP in `Trace.routes`, the LAN of the device as a leg, then the firewall of the device, its host and its reply). Not found: it writes `nat-no-translation` (a drop at the router, no warning), the outcome is `timeout`, no device is reached and the trace has no `translation`, `routes` or `rttMs`.
- `ctx.delivered` (`{ ip, port }`) is what the NAT delivered the packet to. The helpers `headingFor(ctx)` (the IP the packet is heading for on the road: the translated one after the NAT, else `ctx.destIp`) and `arrivalOf(ctx)` (the IP and the packet as the receiver sees them) read it; `ctx.destIp` and `ctx.packet` stay as the sender wrote them, because `Trace.header` and `resolvedIp` show what the sender put on the wire. `host`, `reply` and the inbound side of `firewall-check.ts` use `arrivalOf`, so a rule of the device's firewall and the service lookup see the port of the device and not the public port. The Port Unreachable of a UDP datagram that a forward delivered to a closed port still comes from the public IP the sender used (the game does not model the NAT translating an ICMP error).
- `Trace.notice`, `Trace.hops`, `Trace.header`, `Trace.exchange`, `Trace.routes`, `Trace.translation`, `Trace.dns`, `Trace.question`, `Trace.app` and `Trace.rttMs` are optional: they are absent, not `null` or empty, in every trace without a warning, a router, a sent packet, an answer, a question or a message, and so are the traces of a VPC (the golden test serializes the whole trace). A stage that has nothing to say writes no step.
- A step's `at` names where it happened: a device id, the network (`office` or `vpc`), its edge (`router` or `internet-gateway`) or `internet`. A step of `forward` stops at the sender or at the device that dropped the packet.
- `firewall-check.ts` filters a packet through a device's firewalls for both firewall stages and names their kind in the step. A device without firewalls is not filtered and gets no step.
- `paths/` holds the routers, `traceroute` and the checkpoints of a trace (`stopStep` and `deathPoint`, in `paths/checkpoints.ts`).
- A packet can carry an HTTP message (`PacketSpec.message`, only in TCP: the message is not a packet, it travels in the data of the connection that the handshake opened). `run` seeds `ctx.app = { request }` from it, and `Trace.app` is `{ request, response? }`: the request exists for every packet that carries a message, whether or not the connection opened (it is built from the message alone), and the response exists only when a web service answered. The `app` stage (`stages/web/app.ts`) runs after `host` and before `reply`, only when there is a message: the service that accepted the packet either has a `web` (it chooses a site by the Host header, answers and writes two steps, `web-site-chosen` or `web-default-site`, then `web-answered`) or has none, and then the trip ends as `arrived-no-reply` with the connection open. The reply of the exchange comes back through no firewall. A packet with no message writes no `app` step and has no `Trace.app`. See `src/engine/web/CLAUDE.md`.
- Not here: the stages themselves (`stages/`), the network model (`src/engine/network/`).
