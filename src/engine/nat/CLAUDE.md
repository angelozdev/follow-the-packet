# engine/nat

The NAT of the office: what it does with the source of a packet that leaves, what it does with a packet that comes in from outside, and the table it keeps while a sequence of packets goes on. Pure functions over a table that arrives as an argument and leaves as a result; the stage that calls them is `src/engine/simulation/stages/nat.ts`.

- The NAT is not configured and is not stored in the network: it is derived from the topology (decided 3 of chapter 2). A router that faces a LAN (`facesALan`) and sends a packet out through an interface with an IP that is not private (RFC 1918) translates the source of a packet that a device of the office sent (`publicIpOf`, whose answer is that interface's IP). In the worlds of chapter 1 that is `router-oficina`, whose uplink `203.0.113.10` already existed. The link to another office has a private IP, so it translates nothing. There is no field in `Network` and no action to turn it on: chapter 1 already showed a ping from `192.168.1.10` reaching `198.51.100.50`, and a world that switched the NAT on later would have shown traffic that could not exist.
- `translates(net, router)` is true for the router that is the NAT of the office (it faces a LAN and has an interface with a public IP, the one `publicIpOf` would translate to), which decides which router has a «Tabla de traducciones» card.
- A translation (`Translation`) is `{ protocol, inside, outside, isNew, fixed }`: the end the device sent from, the end the packet leaves the office with, whether this packet made the row and whether the row is a fixed one (a port forward of the router). It is `false` for every packet that leaves, and `true` only for a packet from outside that a forward delivered. It is `Trace.translation`, a field of its own: leaving the office writes no step, reason, hop or leg, and the header (`Trace.header`) and `effectiveSource` keep the private IP, because that is what the sender sent. The view reads the field to show the header outside the office.
- The table (`NatTable`) is a list of rows `{ protocol, inside, outside }` that `translateSource` extends and never changes in place. TCP and UDP make a row; ICMP does not. A ping is translated to the public IP with no ports (an Echo carries an identifier and a sequence number, and it is the identifier that a real NAT translates, RFC 792 and RFC 3022 §2; the game hides it) and leaves no row.
- The mapping is independent of the destination: the same private IP and port reuse their row towards any server, for TCP (RFC 5382 REQ-1, read on 2026-10-11) and for UDP (RFC 4787 REQ-1, read on 2026-10-11). A different protocol, private IP or private port is another row.
- The numbers are the game's, not any system's: public ports start at `FIRST_PUBLIC_PORT` (40001) and each new row takes the next free one, whatever its protocol, so the rows of a sequence are 40001, 40002, 40003 in the order the packets left. The source port is not kept (the example of RFC 3022 §2 changes it too: `10.0.0.10:3017` becomes `138.76.28.4:1024`). A row has no time to live and the table has no limit, so no port is ever reused or runs out. Other NAT choose other numbers, and a real one expires its rows. The takeaway of 02-08 says both limits of the game in plain words: «En un NAT real, las filas caducan; aquí no» and «Una IP pública tiene un número limitado de puertos, y este juego no llega a ese límite» (no figure: the spec declares the exhaustion of ports as hidden and said).
- A row exists from the moment a packet leaves through the router, even if the packet dies later on the road: the translation is made where the packet goes out, not where it ends. A packet the router discards for its TTL, or that finds no route there, was never sent out and makes none.
- **What comes from outside** (decided 17 of chapter 2). A client actor of the internet (`casa-lucia`) sends straight to the public IP of the router of the office; the road between the actor and the router is not modelled, and the TTL goes down once, at that router (`stages/route.ts` names the router that owns the IP and `forward` takes the packet into it). The router is the NAT when it faces a LAN and the IP is a non-private one of its own (`isNatPublicIp`, which asks `publicIpOf`). A TCP or UDP packet to that IP does not get the answer of the router: `translateDestination` looks for where it goes, and only there. A fixed forward comes first (`Router.forwards`, by protocol and public port), then a row of the table that the sends before made (by protocol, public IP and public port, so the row of a TCP exchange does not take a UDP datagram). Whatever it finds, the device and its port (`Delivery.inside`) become what the device sees (`ctx.delivered`) and `Trace.translation` says `{ inside: the device, outside: the public end, isNew: false, fixed }`, with `fixed: true` for a forward. The header, `resolvedIp` and the Port Unreachable of a UDP datagram keep the public end the sender wrote; the device, its firewall and its tcpdump see the translated one.
- `forwardRows(net, router)` lists the forwards of a router as fixed rows `{ protocol, inside: the device's private IP and the port it listens on, outside: the public IP and the public port }`, one for each public IP of the router, in the order of its forwards. It is the same end `translateDestination` finds first, so the table that the view draws (`translationsCard`) lists what delivers instead of working it out again; a forward to a device that is not in the network, or a router that does not translate, gives none.
- **Nothing is delivered without a row or a forward.** The router writes `nat-no-translation` and the outcome is `timeout`: no device is reached, `destinationId` is `null`, and no warning goes back. The game text says «no lo entrega», never that NAT blocks or filters. A forward or a row takes the packet from any sender: the game does not compare who sends with who the row was made for, so a stranger reaches a forward and a packet reaches a row made by a connection it was not part of. A real NAT can restrict that too; it is not modelled, and this note does not say what the RFCs ask. The firewall of the device still runs after the translation (`firewall-in`), and it sees the device's own port: a forward does not filter, a firewall does.
- **A real NAT may answer** an unsolicited SYN, and the game does not (decided D-14 A, 2026-10-11; spec of chapter 2, «Simplificaciones declaradas»). RFC 5382 REQ-4 and REQ-4a, read on 2026-10-11 through a summarizer as the spec records: a NAT must not respond to an unsolicited inbound SYN for at least 6 seconds, and after that it should send an ICMP Port Unreachable, unless its policy is to drop it in silence. `nc -w 3` gives up before 6 seconds, so the player sees a timeout; the text never says the NAT stays silent, only that it does not deliver the packet, and, since 2026-10-12, the feedback of the right option of 02-09 says a real NAT may answer with a notice, but not before 6 seconds, and that `nc` gives up at 3 (the RFC text was re-read in full that day from `https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5382.txt`, REQ-4 and REQ-4a as quoted in `levels/02-ports-and-names/one-address/CLAUDE.md`). That is TCP. The game treats an unsolicited UDP datagram the same way, and what a real NAT does with one has not been verified.
- **A forward's public port is never given to a row.** `translateSource` takes the `reserved` ports and `translateOnExit` passes the public ports of the forwards of the router (whatever their protocol, like the rows), so one outside end never belongs to a forward and to a row at once. A forward looked up first would otherwise hide the row.
- A loose `simulate(net, packet)` knows nothing of the sends before it, so it translates against an empty table: the same packet always gets 40001. `simulateSequence` carries the table from one send to the next (`src/engine/simulation/CLAUDE.md`).
