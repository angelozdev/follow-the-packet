# levels/contract

What an incident is, and what its chips can do: the zod schema, types and constructor of an incident (`incident.ts`: `incidentSchema`, `defineIncident`, `ruleIdFor`, `urlOfChip`, `isGuided`, `settingKind`, `cardsOfPiece` and `firstCardOf`, the flows, tasks, chips, settings, tables, clock, cases and player moves) and the chips (`chips.ts`: the rule two chips name and the chips a rule has (`ruleChipsOf`, `ruleFromChips`, `chipsOfRule`), the packet three send chips make (`packetFromChips`, with the TTL of the message, the port of an `nc`, the datagram of a `dig` or the HTTP request of a `curl` or a browser; `sentPacketFromChips`, the packet a send puts on the wire: the same, and for a `curl` the headers the real tool adds to its request, `User-Agent: curl/8.21.0`, an `Accept` for any type, with `-d` a form-urlencoded `Content-Type` and with `-u` an `Authorization: Basic` that comes first of them and with `-b` a `Cookie` after them, read in the source of curl 8.21.0 on 2026-10-24; the session sends this one, and a browser is marked `browser: true`, so the engine gives it the cookies its device holds, and sends only `Host` and, for a sign-in, the `Content-Type` of the form) or, for a traceroute, the probe it repeats (`probeFromChips`), every combination of send chips (these three take whatever offers send chips, a send incident or a case of a diagnose: `SendOwner`), the check that no combination leaves an office with no routers (a packet to a loopback address stays in the device, so it is no leak), and the checks over ids that name something in a world: the ids a task expects, the devices whose card an identify incident offers, the devices its card that classifies holds, the owners of the tables, the devices of the settings, the ids of the list of shown pieces, where the curls go (`curlsOutsideWebServices`) and the sign-ins the app refuses (`refusedSignIns`); and `routersCrossedByTraceroutes`, the routers every probe of the traceroutes a send owner offers crosses, which the registry compares with the list of shown pieces), the moves on the tables (`tables.ts`: the id of a row of a table, the engine action a move on a table stands for, and the table an incident offers) and the changes a world goes through (`changes.ts`: `changedNames`, the devices, routers and servers of the internet whose configuration differs between two worlds (every field of a device or of a server), whatever the kind of action that made the difference; a piece that only exists in the second world is not a change, and neither is a new interface of a router that faces such a piece (its wiring), while any other change to the interfaces, routes or warnings of that router is; `changedFloors`, the LANs of two or more devices on which every device changed, whose name may stand for those devices; `mentions` and `unnamedChanges`, which ask whether the texts of an incident name them).

- The contract is the only place that says what an incident file may hold; the incident files and the registry read it, they never extend it from outside.
- A filter incident may offer outbound rules: `chips.to` is a second tray of peer chips (the Destination of a rule, same shape as the Source chips of `chips.from`, ids unique and at least one). The move `placeRule` is one for both directions: `{ firewallId, whatId, fromId?, toId?, direction? }`; without `direction` it is inbound and needs `fromId` (and no `toId`), with `outbound` it needs `toId` (and no `fromId`), and the solution may only name chips the incident offers in that tray. `removeRule` takes the same optional `direction`. `ruleIdFor(whatId, peerId, direction)` names an inbound rule `<what>-from-<peer>` and an outbound one `<what>-to-<peer>`, so the same two chips make two different rules; `ruleFromChips` and `chipsOfRule` take the direction too (inbound by default).
- A send task's `expect.outcome` is one of `OUTCOMES` of the engine, `reached` or `any`; its `by` is an id, and the contract cannot say what it names, because the world may come from the incidents before it: the registry test does (`unknownExpectedIds`).
- A message chip is `{ id, label, command?, protocol?, port?, icmpType?, ttl?, options? }`. The `command` fixes the protocol: `packet`, `ping` and `traceroute` are ICMP, `nc`, `curl` and `browser` are TCP and `dig` is UDP; a `protocol` that is written must agree. An ICMP message needs its `icmpType` and has no `port`; an `nc` needs its `port` (1 to 65535) and has no `icmpType`; a `dig` goes to port 53 (its `port`, if written, is 53) and has no `icmpType`; only a `ping` has a `ttl`. A `curl` and a `browser` take their port from the url of the address chip, so they have no `port` or `icmpType`; only a `curl` has `options`, a strict object `{ include?, user?, cookie?, request?, data?, connectTimeout? }` named as the long options of `curl` (`--include` for `-i`, `--user` for `-u`, `--cookie` for `-b`, `--request` for `-X`, `--data` for `-d`, `--connect-timeout`): `include` is `true`, `cookie` is one cookie, `name=value` with no `;` or space (a `-b` with `=` is a cookie string for curl; the file form is not offered), `user` is `user:password` (a user of one character or more, a colon and a password of one character or more: a `-u` with only the user makes `curl` ask for the password, which the game does not model), `request` is `PUT`, `data` is a text of at least one character and `connectTimeout` a positive whole number of seconds. A message with no `command` is ICMP. `packetFromChips` builds the TCP packet of an `nc` with the port of its chip, and the UDP datagram of a `dig` with `lookup: 'dig'`, port 53 and the address chip as its destination. `packetFromChips` builds the TCP packet of a `curl` or a browser from the url of the address chip (`urlOfChip`): the packet goes to the host and the port of that url (80 for `http` and 443 for `https` when it gives none) and carries `message` `{ method, url, body? }`, where `url` is the whole url as the chip gives it (the engine drops the fragment) and the method is `request` of the options when there is one, `POST` when there is `data`, and `GET` otherwise (the browser has no options, so it is a `GET` unless it signs in); the body is `data`. `include` and `connectTimeout` change what the terminal prints and what the box says, never the request; `user` does not change the packet either: `sentPacketFromChips` writes it into the message as the `Authorization` header (`basicAuthorizationHeader`, `@/engine`), before `User-Agent`. A browser message may also have `signIn: { path, user, password }` (only a browser; `path` starts with `/`, user and password are not empty): it sends a `POST` to that path on the host and the port of the address chip it is sent with, with the form of its user in the body (`loginFormOf`, `@/engine`), and its `Content-Type` is the form's; the incident offers one such message for each user the player can sign in as (the label is the name the player reads), and the one browser message with no `signIn` opens an address. `refusedSignIns(owner, world)` lists the combinations of such a message that reach an app which answers 401, a user or a password the app does not list, and the registry asks for none. Nothing is sent for a `curl` or a browser with an address chip that is no url (null). An address chip is `{ id, address }`: any text (an IP, `localhost` or a name), and the contract does not tell them apart, so `officeLeaks` counts an `nc` or a `dig` only when its address is an IP outside the office (a `dig` of a name leaves toward the resolver of the device, which the contract does not read). The same chip may also be a url: `address` is then the host of the url (a name or an IP), and the chip gives either its whole url (`url`) or its parts (`scheme`, `port`, `path`, `query`), never both. By parts, the `scheme` (`http` or `https`) is required, the `path` starts with `/` (the url has `/` when it gives none) and the `query` is written without its `?`; whole, the `url` must be an absolute `http` or `https` url whose host is the `address`. Either way the url that is sent is the same text (`urlOfChip`). The view takes the parts of the strip of the first incident from these chips. `officeLeaks` counts a `curl` or a browser to an IP outside an office with no routers like an `nc`, because its packet is TCP to the host of the url; `incidentTexts` reads the url of each such chip as a text of the incident.
- A send incident may offer settings (`send.settings`): a setting saved on a device, which the player changes on that device's card and not in its terminal. A setting is `{ id, deviceId, kind?, service?, name, chips }` and its `kind` is `gateway` (the kind of a setting that names none, as every setting of chapter 1), `dns-server` or `listen-address`. The chips are `{ id, label, value }`: for a gateway and a DNS server `value` is an IPv4 address or `null` (none), and for a listen address it is `all` or `loopback`, never an IP and never `null`; a value that does not fit its kind is rejected. A listen address names the service it sets, `service: { protocol, port }`, because `setListenAddress` needs both, and the other kinds name none. It has at least two chips, with ids and values that do not repeat (the chip that matches the world is the one whose value is the device's setting), and a gateway or a DNS server belongs to a device that is an origin of the incident (an origin chip whose endpoint is a device; an actor has neither), while a listen address belongs to any device, because the device that listens is usually not the one that sends. The `name` of the tray is a text with a term tag, so the ladder reads it. The move `setSetting` (`{ deviceId, settingId, chipId }`) is part of a send incident's solution, next to `sendPacket`, and names a setting and a chip the incident offers. The contract cannot say whether the device is in the world (the world may come from the incidents before it): the registry does (`unknownSettingDeviceIds`), and `applyMove` leaves a world without that device as it is.
- A send incident may offer tables the player edits (`send.tables`), and a case of a diagnose a fix with the same tables (`fix.tables`). A table is `{ id, kind, ownerId, trays }`. A table of `routes` has two trays, `destination` (chips whose `value` is `default` or a CIDR block) and `nextHop` (the id of a router); a table of `records` has three, `name`, `type` (`A` or `CNAME`, the only records a player edits) and `value`. A tray is a list of chips `{ id, label, value }` with at least one chip and ids that do not repeat; the headers of the columns are the view's, not the contract's (they depend on which terms are known). The TTL of a record is not a tray, because the player does not choose it. The contract cannot say who owns the table: the registry does (`unknownTableOwnerIds`, in the world of each case for a fix). A table of `sites` has a `port` (the TCP port of the web service of its owner) and two trays, `name` (chips whose `value` is a site name under `.example`) and `serves` (chips whose `value` is what a site serves apart from its name: `pages`, `notFoundBody` and, for pages that serve orders, `orders`, the shape of `Site` of the engine without `id` and `names`). The registry's `unknownTableOwnerIds` asks that the owner of a table of sites be a device or a server of the internet with a web service on that port. The moves on a table are `placeEntry` (`{ tableId, picks }`, `picks` by tray name: `destination` and `nextHop` for a route, `name`, `type` and `value` for a record, `name` and `serves` for a site) and `removeEntry` (`{ tableId, entryId }`); they are part of a send incident's solution, next to `sendPacket` and `setSetting`, and the solution may only name a table the incident offers and, for `placeEntry`, exactly one chip of each tray of that table. Whether the row an `entryId` names is in the world the contract cannot say. `tables.ts` turns the move into the engine's action: `placementOf` (a route, a record with the time to live of the game, or a site named after the name picked (its id is that name, so two sites never share one) that serves what was picked, built from the picked chips; null when a pick is missing or is no chip of its tray, or when the chips make no record, an address record whose value is no IP: `chipProblem` says why, `a-record-needs-ip`, so the entry is refused with a reason under the table and never ignored in silence), `removalOf` (the row of the world that has the id; the NS and the SOA of a zone are not rows, and a site is never removed) and `tableOf`. The id of a row is `routeEntryId` (the destination, as the world has it written) for a route and `recordEntryId` (name, type and value, joined) for a record, so a view that draws the rows of a table names them the same way.
- A send incident may have a clock (`send.clock`: at least one step `{ id, label, seconds }`, with ids that do not repeat and a whole, positive number of seconds) whose steps the card «Reloj del juego» offers as «Esperar». The move `wait` (`{ seconds }`, a whole, positive number) is part of a send incident's solution too, and its seconds must be those of a step of the clock.
- A send task may expect, besides the outcome, what the DNS answered: `expect.answer` is `{ ip?, alias? }` (the IP of the answer, and whether it came through an alias; it names at least one) and `expect.status` is `NXDOMAIN` or `SERVFAIL`; it may expect the exact `body` of the response of the web service and which send of its chips it is about, `nth` (a positive whole number: the nth send of that combination since the world last changed; without it, the last). it may also expect the status code of the response, `statusCode` (200, 400 or 404: a code no app of the game answers is refused, from `STATUS_CODES` of the engine). The tasks read them (`src/game/tasks.ts`); the contract only checks their shape. `expect.status` is the status of the DNS, so the HTTP code is `statusCode`.
- A send incident may offer the rules card of one firewall (`send.rules`: `{ firewallId, what, from }`, the Type and Source chips of an inbound rule, ids that do not repeat and at least one chip in each tray; no Destination chips, so only inbound rules). The move `placeRule` is part of a send incident's solution too and may only name that firewall, a `what` chip and a `from` chip of the card, inbound; `ruleChipsOf(incident, firewallId)` gives the chips of a filter incident for every firewall and those of the rules card for its own, `ruleFromChips` builds the rule a placement stands for from them (null for another firewall or a chip the card lacks) and `chipsOfRule` names a rule after them. Whether the firewall is in the world the contract cannot say.
- A send incident may say which card a piece opens first (`send.opensFirst`: the id of a piece of the world and one of `terminal`, `browser`, `settings`, `services`, `routes`, `records` or `sites`). The piece must have that card: the terminal when it is an origin, the browser when it is an origin and the incident offers a message whose command is `browser`, `settings` when a gateway or a DNS server of it is offered, `services` when a listen address of it is, and `routes`, `records` or `sites` when it owns a table of that kind (`cardsOfPiece`). With none, `firstCardOf` gives the first card the piece has in that order. The read-only cards (the translation table, the cache, and the network settings of 02-10, which offer nothing to set) are not contract data and are not in the list: the fichas 02-07 to 02-13 of the plan never need one of them to open first on a piece with more than one card (checked on 2026-10-11); an incident that did would need the registry to say whether the piece has it.
- An identify incident (`identify.cards`) has one card per device; a card's trays have unique ids and unique chip ids, at least two chips each, and a `segmented` tray has exactly two. A tray's `initialPick` names one of its chips, and an `initialPickRule` is only accepted next to an `initialPick`. Every task and every move of the solution names a device, a tray and a chip some card offers, and an identify incident has no flows. The cards' device ids are checked against the world by the registry (`unknownCardDeviceIds`), as `shown` is.
- An identify incident may also have a card that classifies other devices (`identify.classify`, beside the cards): `{ deviceId, cidr, trayId, bins, items }`. `deviceId` is the device whose card it is (the one that opens it), `cidr` the block the items are compared with (a CIDR block, from which the view takes the prefix, the mask and the bits it marks), `bins` exactly two `{ id, name }` with ids that do not repeat (by convention the first holds what fits in the block and the second what does not: the card that classifies reads them in that order), and `items` the devices to sort, at least one and none repeated, none of them the owner. The answer for an item is saved as every answer is, under its own device and under the common `trayId`, and the chip it names is the id of a bin: a task may expect it (`expect: { deviceId: <item>, trayId, chipId: <bin> }`) and a move `answerCard` of the solution may give it. A `trayId` that the card of one of the items already uses is rejected, because the two answers would share a place. The registry checks that the owner and the items are in the world (`unknownClassifiedDeviceIds`), and the list of shown pieces must draw them.
- A diagnose incident (`diagnose.cases`) has no tasks, no solution and none of the incident-level `hints`, `question` and `explanation` (`isGuided` is false for it: it is the type guard of the other three mechanics, which have them). Each case holds an `id` (unique), optional `changes` on the world the incident starts from (never on the case before), a `report`, its own `send` chips (origins, addresses and messages, with the same checks as a send incident's: ids unique per tray, none empty), two `hints` and a `cause`: the `question` and exactly three options, one correct. There is at least one case; the contract does not fix their number. Its flows are ambient only, as a send incident's. A case may carry a `fix` (`{ tables, tasks, solution }`): the tables the player edits to repair it, in the same shape as `send.tables`; the send tasks that must hold to close it, which name only the chips of the case; and its reference `solution`, the moves that repair the world of that case (at least one). The solution is made of `placeEntry` and `removeEntry` on the tables of the fix, with the same checks as a send incident's (`checkTableMove`, shared: a table the fix has and, for `placeEntry`, exactly one chip of each tray), and `sendPacket` of chips the case offers; it has no `setSetting` and no `wait`, because a fix offers neither. It ends with the send of the chips of the tasks, because every change of the world clears the sends and a task reads the last send. The tests replay it through the session, so a fix that cannot be played is caught. Only a case with a fix has one; the only registered one is case 5 of 02-14 (the table of routes of `router-proveedor`, with the reference solution that takes the default route away and writes the block of the servers towards `router-destino`). The tables a move reads are `tablesOf(incident, spec?)`: the incident's `send.tables` or, given a case, only the tables of its fix (`tableOf` and `applyMove` take the case as a last argument, for the session). The moves of the player on a fix come with the session. The registry's checks over ids and leaks (`officeLeaks`, `missingShownIds`) read the chips of every case.
- `shown` is an optional list of the ids of the world (devices, routers and actors, a server of the internet included) that the incident draws; without it the incident draws the whole world, as chapter 4 does. It has at least one id and none repeated. A firewall is not listed: it is drawn with its device. Whether the ids exist in the world, and whether the list holds what the incident uses, are the registry's checks (`unknownShownIds`, and `missingShownIds`: the sender of every flow or origin chip, the owner of every address it sends to, and whoever a task expects, a firewall through its device), because the world may come from the incidents before it. An incident that offers a traceroute must list every router its probes cross (`routersCrossedByTraceroutes`, checked by the registry in the world it starts from and the one its solution leaves, and, for a diagnose, in the world of each case): the traceroute prints the IP of each router that warns, so the map must draw them all. A router that no probe crosses may be listed too (01-10 lists the router in front of the server, which none reaches, and never draws it). A send incident may set `revealRoutersByTraceroute`: its routers, which the list still holds, are drawn only once a traceroute the player sent prints their IP, and the incident must offer a traceroute chip, or they would never appear (`revealsRoutersByTraceroute` reads it; 01-10 is the only one). A case of a diagnose may carry a `shown` list of its own (same shape and same checks, `shownOf`): in a diagnose each case is a screen, so it draws only what it uses, and the incident's list, if it has one, serves only the cases that have none. The registry checks a case's list in the world of that case (`unknownShownIds` and `missingShownIds` take the case): its origin, the owner of each of its addresses and, if it offers a traceroute, every router its probes cross.
